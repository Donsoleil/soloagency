# Legal

Not legal advice. This is a map of what exists, what a template handles, and
where a licensed lawyer is genuinely worth the money. The goal is to stop you
overpaying for the easy parts and underpaying for the dangerous ones.

## The short version of what you actually need

Before taking money: an entity, a bank account in the entity's name, Terms of
Service, and a Privacy Policy. That is the floor, and it is a small list.

Before hiring anyone, including a contractor for a weekend: a written agreement
with IP assignment. This is the one founders skip and the one that blows up
later, because without it the person who wrote your code may own it.

Before you have real revenue: nothing else is urgent. Trademarks, patents, and
elaborate corporate structure are usually premature and consume money you need
for validation.

## Entity

An LLC is simpler, cheaper, and taxed through to you. It fits a bootstrapped
business with no outside investors, which is most solo founders.

A C-corporation, usually Delaware, is what institutional investors require and
it brings double taxation plus real filing overhead. Choose it if you are
actually raising, not because startup writing says to.

You can convert later. Founders spend weeks on this decision and the cost of
being wrong is lower than the cost of the delay.

Whatever you pick, keep business and personal money separate from day one.
Mixing them is what undoes the liability protection you formed the entity for,
which makes it the cheapest mistake to avoid and a common one.

## Templates versus a lawyer

Templates are fine, with review, for: Terms of Service and Privacy Policy on a
standard SaaS, contractor agreements, NDAs, and simple customer terms. Use a
reputable source, and read every line rather than pasting, because generated
policies routinely promise things your product does not do.

Pay a lawyer for: anything with equity, anything involving a co-founder,
customer contracts a counterparty drafted, regulated industries, and anything
you would not survive being wrong about. A couple of hours of a specialist's
time here is cheap relative to the exposure.

## Privacy Policy, specifically

It has to describe what you actually collect, which means writing it after you
know your data flows, not before. A policy claiming practices you do not follow
is worse than a plain honest one, because it is a written commitment you are
already breaking.

## Reversibility

Entity choice is reversible with effort. Signatures, equity grants, and
anything with a counterparty are one-way. Slow down for those and get a real
read.

## Evidence

A checklist with each item marked done, templated pending review, or not needed
yet with a reason, plus a named point where a lawyer is required. Unfilled
placeholder text in a published policy is a fail, not a detail.
