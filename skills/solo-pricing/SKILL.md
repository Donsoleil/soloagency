# Pricing

Price is positioning before it is revenue. It tells a buyer who the product is
for, and they read it before they read your features.

## Read first

If Stripe, Square, or PayPal is connected, pull actual numbers before
recommending anything: current prices, plan distribution, refund and churn rate
by plan, and the discounts actually being given. Founders misremember their own
pricing constantly, especially the discounts. If no connector is available, ask
for a plan list and a rough split, and say the analysis is founder-reported.

## Step 1: the value metric

The value metric is the unit you charge for. It needs to be predictable to the
buyer, and it needs to grow as they get more value. Get this wrong and no tier
structure saves you.

| If the product is | Charge by |
|---|---|
| A collaboration tool | Seats |
| A developer tool | Usage: calls, builds, compute |
| A marketing tool | Contacts, sends, or campaigns |
| A service replacement | Outcome delivered: per filing, per job, per report |
| A single-operator tool | Flat, because seats will always equal one |

That last row is the one solo founders miss. Seat pricing on a product used by
one person per company is a growth ceiling you built yourself.

## Step 2: tiers

Three is the default because it does three jobs: the top tier anchors, the
middle sells, the bottom removes the price objection. Design the middle tier to
be the obvious answer and make the boundary between tiers something the customer
can feel, not a feature-count table.

If the top tier never sells, it is not priced too high, it is not differentiated
enough. Add capability rather than cutting price.

## Step 3: the number

Anchor from three inputs, in this order of weight: what the alternative costs
them today including their own hours, competitor prices from
`solo-market-research`, and your income floor divided by realistic customer
count. The cost-of-alternative number is the one buyers actually respond to,
because it is their number, not yours.

Solo founders underprice, consistently and badly. The tell is a high close rate
with no negotiation: if nobody ever pushes back on price, you are below the
market. Some friction at the point of sale is the correct amount.

## Free trial versus freemium

Free trial when time is what the buyer needs to see value, and when the product
costs you money per user. Freemium when the free tier creates something you
benefit from, usage data, content, invitations, or network. Freemium with
neither is a support burden with no upside. For most single-operator tools,
trial wins.

## Raising prices: this one is not reversible

New customers only, by default. Grandfather existing ones and say so publicly,
because it costs you little and buys goodwill you cannot buy back later. If you
must raise on existing accounts, give long notice, explain it once without
apologizing, and expect churn concentrated in the cheapest cohort, which is
usually fine.

## Evidence

The value metric with a one-line reason, the tier table with prices, the
cost-of-alternative number that anchors it, and either a connector-pulled
current state or an explicit note that current pricing is founder-reported.
