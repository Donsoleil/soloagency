# Community

A community is a recurring obligation, not an asset you launch. For one person
it is worth it only for a specific reason, and the honest default answer is not
yet.

## The honest gate

Answer these before starting. Do your customers already have a place they
gather, and is it not yours? Then go there instead, and be useful in it. Do you
have enough active users that any given day has a conversation in it? Under
roughly a hundred engaged users, a room stays visibly empty and empty rooms
signal decline. Can you show up most days for months? An abandoned community
is worse for your product than none, because it is public evidence of
abandonment.

If any answer is no, do not start one. Say so plainly rather than helping build
a room that will embarrass them in three months.

## The reason that justifies it

One of: peer support that genuinely reduces your ticket load, a feedback loop
tighter than surveys, or visible proof for prospects that other people use this.
"Engagement" is not a reason. Pick the one and design for it, because the
platform and the norms follow from the reason.

## Platform

Match where the segment already is rather than what you like. Chat platforms
give liveliness and lose all knowledge to scrollback, which is a real cost if
support reduction is the reason. Forums are slower and searchable, so the same
answer keeps working. If the reason is cutting support load, searchable wins
and the choice is not close.

## Launching without an empty room

Start with a small invited group, ten to thirty of your most engaged people,
before anyone public. Seed the real questions yourself. Answer everything
quickly for the first few weeks, because response time sets the norm more than
any rules document. Open it up only once conversations happen without you
starting them.

## Keeping it from eating the week

Set the hours you show up, and say them publicly. Promote two or three people
who are already answering things. Write down the three answers you repeat most
and pin them, which converts community work into support reduction. If it is
consuming your build time with no attributable return, closing it deliberately,
with an explanation and an archive, is a legitimate outcome rather than a
failure.

## Evidence

The gate answered honestly with the engaged-user count, the one reason stated,
and for a running community: weekly active posters, share of questions answered
by someone other than you, and the support-ticket trend. "It feels active" is
`NOT VERIFIED`.
